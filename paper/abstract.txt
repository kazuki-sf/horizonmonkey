A long-horizon agent inherits organizational memory it did not write: lessons consolidated and re-summarized by earlier sessions of itself. Giving every record a provenance link does not make that memory safe, because an agent holding hundreds of inherited beliefs can follow only a few links before it acts. The question is not whether provenance exists but which links get followed. In a scenario where one of six inherited memories has lost a true negative caveat and the agent may pull at most k source records before committing, allocation is governed by the agent's own plan: across 1020 pre-registered episodes the corrupted memory is verified in 236/236 episodes whose first-pass intent it backs and 464/784 otherwise, with no counterexample. Randomizing which source records are carried into a later decision, after the budget is spent and the situation has shifted, shows this is causal: 139/150 episodes chose the corrupted direction without that provenance in context against 3/150 with it, and every unguarded commitment to it (39/150 vs 0/150) is on the side that never saw the caveat. A second identification strategy agrees within a point. Matched-qualifier controls reverse our own reading of the mechanism: with length and hedging controlled, a stated caveat suppresses verification rather than attracting it, and a memory hedging about something irrelevant is checked more often than one whose material caveat was silently deleted. Finally we test the premise this threat model rests on by running a real consolidation chain, and it fails: over six generations quantified negatives mostly survive while scope and prohibition erode, and a body carrying no negative content appears in 0 of 360 chain-generations. The allocation failure is real and causally identified; ordinary summarization is not what produces the belief that triggers it. Hypotheses and scoring were committed before any model call.
