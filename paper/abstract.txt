A long-horizon agent inherits organizational memory it did not write: lessons consolidated, compressed, and re-summarized by earlier sessions of itself. It cannot verify all of them against their sources, so its reliability depends on an allocation decision current evaluations do not measure: which inherited beliefs get checked when checking is scarce. We construct a controlled scenario in which one of six inherited memories has lost a true, materially negative caveat during consolidation, give the agent a budget of source-record lookups, and measure where the budget goes. Across 1,470 pre-registered episodes spanning six production models from two providers, with memory order randomized and length balanced: (1) verification is intent-aligned -- 65% of scarce lookups go to memories backing the action the model already intends (uniform expectation 30%), and the corrupted high-consequence belief is checked far more often when it backs that intention than when it does not; (2) deleting the caveat makes the memory more likely to be checked, but the reallocation varies from near-always to near-never across models at the same task; (3) a one-sentence triage instruction raises verification without closing the gap. A second experiment shifts the situation mid-session, after the budget is spent, so the corrupted lesson becomes load-bearing: pre-registered strict failure metrics return zero (self-reported hedging saturates), but exploratorily, episodes whose earlier budget recovered the lost caveat choose the corrupted direction 6% of the time versus 79% without it, in every model, and the triage instruction, randomized across arms, halves the behavioral rate one decision later. Stated evidence ratings and allocation dissociate in both directions. All hypotheses and scoring were committed before any model call; everything reproduces from released episode files.
